# Miscible

**Summary**: Two liquids are miscible if they mix completely in all proportions to form a homogeneous solution (e.g., ethanol and water). Immiscible liquids do not mix and form separate layers (e.g., oil and water).

**Created**: 2026-07-14
**Last Updated**: 2026-07-14

---

**Miscible** describes two liquids that dissolve in one another in all proportions to form a single, homogeneous layer, with no visible boundary between them — ethanol and water are the most commonly cited miscible pair in IGCSE chemistry. The miscibility of two liquids depends on their molecular polarity: "like dissolves like" — polar solvents such as water mix readily with other polar liquids (e.g., ethanol, propanone) because the intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonds, dipole-dipole attractions) between the different molecules are comparable in strength to those within each pure liquid. **Immiscible** liquids, by contrast, do not mix and form two distinct layers, as seen with oil and water, because the non-polar oil molecules cannot form favourable interactions with the highly polar water molecules. The practical importance of miscibility lies in separation technique selection: miscible liquids with different boiling points are separated by fractional distillation (exploiting boiling point differences), whereas immiscible liquids are separated using a separating funnel, simply by draining the denser lower layer and collecting the upper layer separately. IGCSE candidates should be prepared to identify whether fractional distillation or a separating funnel is the appropriate apparatus based solely on whether two liquids are described as miscible or immiscible.
