# Rusting of Iron

**Summary**: Rusting requires BOTH oxygen and water. Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide (Fe2O3.xH2O) -- flaky and porous, so iron rusts completely away. Prevention: barrier methods (paint, oil, plastic, tin-plating), sacrificial protection (galvanising with zinc, Mg or Zn blocks), and alloying (stainless steel with Cr + Ni).

## What Is Rust?

**Rust** = hydrated iron(III) oxide, formula **Fe2O3.xH2O**, where x is a variable number of water molecules.

Rust is NOT simply iron oxide (Fe2O3). The presence of water in the crystal structure is what makes rust different.

The simplified chemical equation for rusting can be written as:
- 4Fe(s) + 3O2(g) + 2xH2O(l) -> 2Fe2O3.xH2O(s)

## Conditions for Rusting

**Both oxygen AND water must be present for rusting to occur.** If either one is absent, rusting does not happen.

**Classic experiment -- iron nails in test tubes:**

| Test Tube Conditions | Does Rust Form? | Explanation |
|---------------------|-----------------|-------------|
| Nail in **dry air** (anhydrous CaCl2 absorbs moisture) | No | Water is absent |
| Nail in **boiled water + oil layer** (boiling removes dissolved O2; oil prevents air entry) | No | Oxygen is absent |
| Nail in **water exposed to air** (both water and air present) | **Yes** | Both O2 and H2O present |
| Nail in **salt water exposed to air** | Yes -- **faster** | NaCl acts as an electrolyte |

A painted nail control: a fourth tube has a nail coated with paint/oil -- this nail does NOT rust because the barrier prevents O2 and H2O from reaching the iron.

## Factors That Accelerate Rusting

| Factor | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| **Salt** (NaCl) | Salt acts as an **electrolyte**, increasing the rate of ion movement and thus the electrochemical corrosion process. Cars rust faster in coastal areas and in countries where roads are salted in winter. |
| **Acid rain** (SO2, NOx) | Acidic conditions increase the rate of corrosion. Acid rain from sulfur dioxide (SO2) and nitrogen oxides (NOx) accelerates rusting. |
| **Impurities in iron** | Pure iron rusts very slowly. Iron with impurities (e.g., from the blast furnace) rusts faster because the impurities act as cathodic sites, enabling electrochemical corrosion. |
| **Higher temperatures** | Increases the rate of reaction (kinetic particle theory). |

## Why Rusting Is a Serious Problem

**Rust vs iron -- comparing the two:**

| Property | Iron | Rust (Fe2O3.xH2O) |
|----------|------|--------------------|
| Physical form | Solid, strong metal | **Flaky, crumbly, porous** solid |
| Adhesion to iron | -- | **Does not stick** to the surface |
| Volume | Dense | **Greater volume** -- rust occupies more space than the iron it replaces |
| Protective? | -- | **No** -- it flakes off, exposing fresh iron beneath |

Because rust is **flaky and porous**:
- It **falls off** the surface, exposing **fresh iron** to air and water.
- This fresh iron then rusts too.
- The process repeats until the entire piece of iron is **completely rusted away**.

This is why rusting is so destructive -- unlike the Al2O3 layer on aluminium (which adheres strongly and protects), rust does not form a protective layer. It **never stops**.

For every 1 cm3 of iron that rusts, about 8 cm3 of rust is produced. This expansion cracks concrete (reinforced concrete structures fail when internal steel rebars rust and expand).

## Methods of Rust Prevention

### Barrier Protection

A coating is applied to prevent oxygen and water from reaching the iron.

| Method | How It Works | Examples | Limitation |
|--------|-------------|----------|------------|
| **Painting** | Paint forms a waterproof layer | Bridges, car bodies, ships, window frames | If paint is scratched, iron beneath is exposed and rusts |
| **Oiling/greasing** | Oil/grease forms a waterproof film | Moving engine parts, tools, bicycle chains | Needs reapplication; can attract dirt |
| **Plastic coating** | Plastic sheath prevents contact | Dish racks, wire fences, coat hangers, electrical cable armour | If cracked, water can enter |
| **Tin-plating** | Thin layer of tin electroplated onto steel | Steel food cans ("tin cans") | If scratched, **iron rusts faster** (see below) |
| **Electroplating** (with Cr, Ni) | Thin protective/decorative layer | Car bumpers, bathroom fittings | If scratched, iron exposed unless the plating metal is more reactive |

**Why tin-plating is a problem if scratched:**
Tin (Sn) is **below** iron in the reactivity series. If the tin layer is scratched:
- The exposed iron acts as the **anode** (more reactive).
- The tin acts as the **cathode**.
- In the presence of moisture (electrolyte), the iron corrodes **faster** than it would without tin, because of the electrochemical cell formed between the two metals.

### Sacrificial Protection

A **more reactive metal** is attached to or coats the iron. This more reactive metal corrodes **in preference** to the iron.

| Method | How It Works | Details |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Galvanising** (zinc coating) | Zinc layer + sacrificial protection | Zinc is MORE reactive than iron. Coating is applied by hot-dip galvanising (dipping in molten zinc) or electroplating. |
| **Magnesium blocks** | Attached to hulls of ships, underground pipelines, oil rigs | Mg is more reactive than Fe; Mg corrodes sacrificially and is periodically replaced |
| **Zinc blocks** | Attached to ships, steel structures in seawater | Same principle as Mg blocks; zinc is more reactive than iron |

**How galvanising works even when scratched:**
Even if the zinc coating is scratched, the iron underneath does NOT rust because:
1. Zinc is **more reactive** than iron (zinc is above iron in the reactivity series).
2. Zinc loses electrons more readily: Zn -> Zn2+ + 2e- (zinc oxidises in preference).
3. The iron acts as the **cathode** and is protected as long as any zinc remains.
4. The electrons flow from zinc (sacrificed) to iron (protected).

This is why galvanised dustbins, roofing sheets, and lamp posts last for decades with scratches -- the zinc sacrifices itself.

**Sacrificial blocks on ships:**
- Blocks of magnesium or zinc are bolted to the steel hull of a ship below the waterline.
- The blocks (more reactive than Fe) corrode instead of the steel hull.
- The blocks are replaced periodically once they are nearly consumed.
- This is much cheaper than repairing or replacing the ship's hull.

### Alloying

**Stainless steel** (Fe + Cr + Ni + C) resists rusting because the chromium forms a thin, tough, invisible layer of **chromium(III) oxide (Cr2O3)** on the surface. This layer is:
- Impermeable to O2 and H2O.
- Strongly adherent (does not flake off).
- Self-healing (reforms if scratched).

## Electrochemical Explanation (Extension)

Rusting is an **electrochemical process** that can be understood in terms of a simple voltaic cell:

1. At the **anode** (site of oxidation): Fe -> Fe2+ + 2e-
   - Iron atoms lose electrons and enter the water as Fe2+ ions.
2. Electrons flow through the iron to a **cathode** (an impurity site or another region).
3. At the **cathode** (site of reduction): O2 + 2H2O + 4e- -> 4OH-
   - Dissolved oxygen in water is reduced to hydroxide ions.
4. Fe2+ and OH- combine: Fe2+ + 2OH- -> Fe(OH)2, which is further oxidised by O2 to form Fe2O3.xH2O (rust).

**Impurities accelerate rusting** because they provide extra cathodic sites where the reduction half-reaction can occur.

## Key Points

- Rust = hydrated iron(III) oxide = Fe2O3.xH2O
- Rusting requires BOTH oxygen AND water
- Experiment: anhydrous CaCl2 (dry air, no H2O) = no rust; boiled water + oil (no O2) = no rust; water + air = rust
- Salt, acid rain, and impurities accelerate rusting
- Rust is flaky and porous -> falls off -> exposes fresh iron -> iron completely rusts away
- Al2O3 layer on Al is protective (adherent, impermeable); rust on Fe is NOT protective (flaky)
- Barrier methods: paint, oil, plastic, tin-plating, electroplating
- Galvanising = zinc coating -> sacrificial protection (Zn more reactive than Fe)
- Tin is less reactive than Fe -> if scratched, Fe rusts faster
- Sacrificial anodes: Mg or Zn blocks on ships, pipelines, oil rigs
- Stainless steel = Fe + Cr + Ni -> Cr forms protective Cr2O3 layer

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Rust is just iron oxide (Fe2O3)" | Rust is **hydrated** iron(III) oxide (Fe2O3.xH2O). The water molecules are part of the structure. |
| "Water alone causes rusting" | Both O2 AND H2O are required. Boiled water with an oil layer (no O2) does not cause rusting. |
| "Iron rusts because the oxide layer is damaged" | Iron oxide (rust) is inherently flaky and non-adherent. Even an intact layer of rust does not protect -- it flakes off. |
| "Tin-plating and galvanising both protect the same way" | Tin provides only barrier protection; zinc provides both barrier AND sacrificial protection because zinc is more reactive than iron. |
| "Sacrificial protection means coating with any other metal" | The coating metal must be MORE reactive than iron. Tin and copper are less reactive and make rusting worse if scratched. |
| "Stainless steel never rusts" | Under very harsh conditions (prolonged exposure to chlorides, e.g., in seawater), even stainless steel can corrode by pitting. It is resistant, not immune. |
