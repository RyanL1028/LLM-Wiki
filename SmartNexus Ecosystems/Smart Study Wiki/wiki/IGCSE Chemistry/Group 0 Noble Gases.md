# Group 0 Noble Gases

**Summary**: Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn) are monatomic, colourless, and unreactive due to full outer electron shells. Uses: He in balloons, Ar in welding, Ne in advertising signs.

## Why Noble Gases Are Unreactive

The noble gases (Group 0, also called Group 18) have a **full outer shell of electrons**. This makes them **very stable** — they do not need to gain, lose, or share electrons to achieve a stable configuration because they already have one.

- Helium (He): 2 electrons in its only shell → full (the first shell can only hold 2 electrons)
- Neon (Ne): 2.8 → full outer shell (8 electrons)
- Argon (Ar): 2.8.8 → full outer shell (8 electrons)

Because they have full outer shells, noble gases:
- Are **unreactive** (formerly called **"inert gases"** — though a few compounds of krypton and xenon have now been made, requiring extreme conditions, proving they are not completely inert)
- Do **not readily form compounds** under normal conditions
- Exist as **monatomic gases** — single atoms, not bonded to other atoms (unlike most other gases which are diatomic, e.g., O2, N2, Cl2)

**Example**: Neon has a full outer shell of electrons (electron configuration 2.8). It does not need to gain, lose, or share electrons to achieve a stable configuration, so it does not form bonds.

## Physical Properties and Electron Configurations

Noble gases are all **colourless, odourless gases at room temperature**. They are monatomic (exist as single atoms: He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn — NOT He2, Ne2 etc.).

**Electron configurations of the first five noble gases:**

| Element | Symbol | Atomic Number | Electron Configuration |
|---------|--------|--------------|----------------------|
| Helium | He | 2 | **2** |
| Neon | Ne | 10 | **2.8** |
| Argon | Ar | 18 | **2.8.8** |
| Krypton | Kr | 36 | 2.8.18.8 |
| Xenon | Xe | 54 | 2.8.18.18.8 |

**Trend in boiling point down the group:**

Boiling points **increase** down Group 0.

| Noble Gas | Boiling Point (°C) |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Helium (He) | -269 |
| Neon (Ne) | -246 |
| Argon (Ar) | -186 |
| Krypton (Kr) | -153 |
| Xenon (Xe) | -108 |

**Explanation**: As you go down the group, the atoms get larger (more electrons, more shells). The **intermolecular forces** (van der Waals forces) between the atoms become **stronger** because there are more electrons to create temporary dipoles. More energy is needed to overcome these forces, so boiling points increase.

**Trend in density:**
Density also **increases** down the group because atomic mass increases faster than atomic volume.

**Example**: Xenon atoms are larger and have more electrons than helium atoms. The intermolecular forces between xenon atoms are stronger. More energy is required to overcome these forces, giving xenon a higher boiling point.

## Uses of Noble Gases

The uses of noble gases are directly linked to their **properties**.

| Noble Gas | Use(s) | Property That Makes It Suitable |
|-----------|--------|-------------------------------|
| **Helium (He)** | Filling **balloons and airships** | **Low density** (lighter than air — only hydrogen is lighter, but hydrogen is flammable; helium is not); **non-flammable / unreactive** (safer than hydrogen, which caused the Hindenburg disaster) |
| Helium | **Coolant** for superconducting magnets (e.g., in MRI scanners) | Very **low boiling point** (-269 °C); can reach extremely low temperatures needed for superconductivity |
| Helium | Deep-sea diving gas (mixed with oxygen — heliox) | Low solubility in blood (reduces risk of nitrogen narcosis / "the bends") |
| **Neon (Ne)** | **Advertising signs / neon lights** | Emits a characteristic **red-orange glow** when an electric current is passed through it at low pressure; other colours are made by mixing neon with other gases or using coloured glass tubes |
| **Argon (Ar)** | **Welding** (as a shielding gas, e.g., in TIG welding) | **Unreactive / inert** — provides an inert atmosphere that prevents the hot metal from reacting with oxygen or nitrogen in the air |
| Argon | Filling **light bulbs / filament lamps** | **Unreactive** — prevents the tungsten filament from oxidising (burning) at high temperatures, extending the bulb's life |
| **Krypton (Kr)** | High-intensity lamps (e.g., airport runway lights, laser shows) | Emits a bright white/blue light when electricity passes through; can produce high-intensity beams |
| **Xenon (Xe)** | **Flash photography** (xenon flash lamps); **car headlamps** (HID — high-intensity discharge); lasers | Produces an extremely bright, short burst of white light when an electric current passes through; very high luminous efficiency |
| Radon (Rn) | (No practical uses — it is radioactive and a health hazard; can accumulate in basements from uranium decay in rocks) | N/A |

**Why the uses of helium are particularly important:**
Helium is **non-renewable** — it is extracted from natural gas deposits, and once released, it escapes Earth's atmosphere. Its unique combination of low density, unreactivity, and very low boiling point makes it irreplaceable in many applications. Helium is preferred over hydrogen for balloons: **hydrogen is flammable**; helium is not.

## Discovery of the Noble Gases

**Why they were discovered late:**
- Noble gases are **unreactive** — they do not form compounds under normal conditions.
- Most elements were discovered through their reactions or the compounds they form (e.g., metals isolated from their ores, gases identified through chemical tests).
- Since noble gases do not react, there were no obvious signs of their existence — no coloured compounds, no precipitates, no flame tests that would reveal their presence.
- They were only discovered when scientists began to notice small discrepancies in the density of "nitrogen" extracted from air vs nitrogen made chemically.

**Historical timeline:**
- **1894**: **Argon** discovered by Lord Rayleigh and Sir William Ramsay. Rayleigh noticed that "nitrogen" from air (after removing O2, CO2, and water vapour) was slightly denser than nitrogen made from chemical reactions. The difference was due to about 1% argon in the air.
- **1895**: **Helium** was first identified on Earth by Ramsay (it had previously been detected in the Sun's spectrum — hence its name from the Greek "helios" meaning sun).
- **1898**: **Neon, krypton, and xenon** discovered by Ramsay and Morris Travers through the fractional distillation of liquid air.
- **1900**: **Radon** discovered by Friedrich Ernst Dorn.

## Key Points

- Group 0 (Group 18) = **noble gases**: He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn
- All have a **full outer shell** → **unreactive** (formerly called inert gases)
- All exist as **monatomic gases** (single atoms: He, not He2)
- Electron configurations: **He = 2, Ne = 2.8, Ar = 2.8.8**
- Boiling point **increases** down group (stronger intermolecular forces with larger atoms)
- Density **increases** down the group
- **Helium**: balloons/airships (low density, non-flammable); coolant (very low bp)
- **Neon**: advertising signs (glows red-orange with electricity)
- **Argon**: welding, light bulbs (provides inert atmosphere)
- Discovered late because they are unreactive — no compounds signalled their presence

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Noble gases are diatomic like other gases" | Noble gases are **monatomic** — they exist as single atoms (He, Ne, Ar, etc.), not He2, Ne2. All other common gases (O2, N2, H2, Cl2) are diatomic. |
| "Helium has 8 electrons in its outer shell" | Helium has only **2 electrons** total, occupying the first shell, which is full. 8 outer electrons is only for the second shell onwards. |
| "Noble gases are completely inert and never form compounds" | They are **extremely unreactive**, but compounds of xenon and krypton have been made under extreme conditions (e.g., XeF4). For IGCSE purposes, they are considered unreactive. |
| "Argon is used in balloons" | **Helium** is used in balloons. Argon is used for welding and light bulbs. |
| "Neon signs are made of many different neon-coloured gases" | Different colours in "neon signs" are produced by different gases or phosphor coatings — pure neon glows **red-orange** only. Other colours use argon (blue), krypton (white), or mercury vapour (blue-green) with coloured glass. |
