# Polymers

## What is a Polymer?

A **polymer** is a long-chain molecule made from many small repeating units called **monomers**. Polymerisation = joining monomers together.

## Addition Polymerisation

Monomers with **C=C double bonds** join together. The double bond opens up — no other products are formed.

### Examples:

**Poly(ethene):**
n(CH₂=CH₂) → —(CH₂—CH₂)—ₙ
- Monomer: ethene (C₂H₄)
- Uses: plastic bags, bottles, cling film

**Poly(propene):**
n(CH₃CH=CH₂) → —(CH(CH₃)—CH₂)—ₙ
- Uses: ropes, carpets, food containers

**PVC (poly(chloroethene)):**
n(CH₂=CHCl) → —(CH₂—CHCl)—ₙ
- Uses: pipes, window frames, electrical insulation

**PTFE (poly(tetrafluoroethene)):**
n(CF₂=CF₂) → —(CF₂—CF₂)—ₙ
- Uses: non-stick coatings (Teflon ®)

### Drawing Repeating Units
- Remove the C=C double bond
- Extend single bonds outward (shown as continuation bonds)
- Put brackets around the repeating unit with 'n' subscript

## Condensation Polymerisation

Monomers join with the **elimination of a small molecule** (usually water or HCl).

### Nylon (polyamide)
- Monomers: dicarboxylic acid + diamine
- Amide linkage: —CONH—
- Water eliminated

### PET / Polyester
- Monomers: dicarboxylic acid + diol
- Ester linkage: —COO—
- Water eliminated

## Plastic Disposal Issues

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|--------|-----------|---------------|
| Landfill | Cheap | Land use, non-biodegradable |
| Incineration | Energy recovery | Toxic gases (HCl from PVC, CO) |
| Recycling | Conserves resources | Sorting difficult, quality degrades |
| Biodegradable plastics | Breakdown naturally | More expensive, weaker |

## Key Facts
- Addition polymerisation: C=C opens, no other products
- Condensation polymerisation: small molecule eliminated
- Poly(ethene) from ethene; PVC from chloroethene
- Non-biodegradable plastics persist in the environment
