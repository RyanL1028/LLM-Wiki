### How are elements organized?

In total, about 100 elements exist naturally. Each element has its own properties, so it would be difficult to remember them all.

Fortunately, there are patterns in the properties of elements. Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev used these patterns to create the Periodic Table. The modern Periodic Table lists all the elements that scientists know. It groups together elements with similar properties. Figure 2 shows the Periodic Table.

![The Periodic Table of elements. There is a red line which runs to the left of the elements boron, silicon, arsenic, tellurium, astatine and oganesson.](images/9781382063821_graphic_240.svg)

### Metal or non-metal?

There are several ways of sorting, or classifying, elements. One classification has two categories: metals and non-metals. In the Periodic Table, metals are on the left of the red line in Figure 2. Non-metals are on the right. Figure 3 shows a metal – silver. Figure 4 shows a non-metal – sulfur.

Most metals have high melting points. They are usually solid at 20 °C. Many non-metals have low boiling points. For example, oxygen and chlorine are in the gas state at 20 °C.

Melting and boiling points, and the properties in Table 1, are **physical properties**. They describe properties you can see or measure without changing the material.

### Do all elements fit the pattern?

Most elements are easy to group as metals or non-metals. But the system is not perfect. Mercury is a metal, but is in the liquid state at 20 °C. One form of the non-metal element carbon, graphite, is a good conductor of electricity.

The elements near the red line in the Periodic Table, like germanium and silicon, are **metalloids**. Their properties are between those of metals and non-metals. Most metalloids are shiny. They are semiconductors of electricity. This means they are between conductors and insulators.
