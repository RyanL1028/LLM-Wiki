### Changes of state

When a substance changes from the solid to liquid state, it melts. **Melting** is the change from solid to liquid. **Freezing** is the change from liquid to solid.

Melting and freezing are examples of **[[Changes of State]]**. Changes of state are **reversible**. For example, ice melts to make liquid water, but ice forms again if the water is cooled to 0°C.

### Explaining melting and freezing

What happens when an ice cube melts? The surroundings (environment around an object) transfer [[Energy]] to the ice. This makes its particles vibrate (shake) faster. The particles move away from their places in the pattern (see Figure 2). The particles continue to move around. As more particles leave the pattern, more ice melts.

![Two boxes showing particles before and after melting. Before melting, the particles are tightly arranged in a regular pattern. After melting, the particles still touch each other but they are loosely arranged in an irregular pattern.](images/9781382063821_graphic_180.svg)

*Figure 2Particles before and after melting.*

When a liquid starts to freeze, its particles move more slowly as they transfer energy to their surroundings. The particles get into a pattern and vibrate on the spot. Eventually, all the liquid freezes.

No particles are added or removed when a substance melts or freezes. This means that its mass does not change. Scientists say that mass is conserved when a substance melts.

### What is a melting point?

The temperature at which a substance melts is its melting point. Melting points give us information about the states of substances at different temperatures.
- Below its melting point, a substance is in the solid state.
- Above its melting point, a substance may be in the liquid or gas state. You need more data to work out which.

Look at the data in Table 1. At room temperature (20°C), gallium and gold are below their melting points. At 20°C, gallium and gold are solid. Above 30°C, gallium is liquid. This is why gallium will melt on your hands (see Figure 3).

At 20°C, oxygen is above its melting point, so it must be in the liquid or gas state. However, from the data in Table 1, you cannot tell which of these two states it is in.

Gold has a higher melting point than water. This means that the particles in solid gold hold together more strongly than the particles in solid water (ice).
